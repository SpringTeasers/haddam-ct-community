Haddam, Connecticut

About Haddam

Haddam is a town in Middlesex County, Connecticut, and it is the only town in the state that the Connecticut River runs through the middle of rather than along its edge.

Residents live on both banks. On the west side are the villages of Higganum, Shailerville, Tylerville and Hidden Lake; on the east side is Haddam Neck. The town was formally established in 1668 and had a population of 8,452 at the 2020 census.

Haddam is part of the Lower Connecticut River Valley Planning Region. It is a largely residential community with an excellent school system, acres of recreational open space, and a strong interest in its own history.

Where Haddam is

Haddam covers about 46 square miles — roughly 44 square miles of land and just over 2 square miles of water — in south-central Connecticut in the lower Connecticut River Valley. The town sits within the Lower Connecticut River Valley Planning Region, and part of Cockaponset State Forest lies within its borders.

Five villages, one town

Because the river divides Haddam, the town grew as a set of distinct villages rather than one single center. The town’s own account of its history names Haddam, Higganum, Haddam Neck and Tylerville among its distinct villages; the town also contains Hidden Lake and Shailerville on the west side of the river.

  • Higganum was settled between 1685 and 1700 and was first called “Hegamumpus,” meaning “at axe rock.” Higganum Landing became a successful river port and shipbuilding center, and in the 19th century Higganum was the town’s industrial heart.
  • Haddam Neck lies on the east bank and was settled around 1712, remaining part of Haddam.
  • Haddam village, on the west bank, is where the original English settlement was made, in the area of today’s Haddam village and Shailerville.
  • Shailerville and Tylerville were settled in the late 17th century and became home to shipbuilders, mariners, merchants and ferrymen.
  • Hidden Lake is the fifth village, on the west side of the river.

Later-settled parts of town included Ponsett, Candlewood Hill, Little City, Burr and Turkey Hill. Higganum and Tylerville remain viable commercial centers for the town today.

A town that grew up on the river

Before the English

The earliest residents of the Haddam area were members of the Wangunk tribe, who hunted, fished and grew corn, beans and tobacco. The Wangunks had numerous settlements in the area and generally peaceful relationships with neighboring tribes.

The purchase and the founding

In 1662, representatives for the Connecticut Colony bought from the Wangunks the land the English called the “Plantation at Thirty Mile Island” — 104 square miles, extending six miles inland on both sides of the Connecticut River and covering all of what is now Haddam and East Haddam. The English paid 30 coats, worth roughly $100 at the time, and kept Thirty Mile Island for themselves along with the right to fish and hunt where they pleased. The name came from the English belief that the island lay 30 miles north of the river’s mouth; in fact it is only about 17 miles from Long Island Sound.

In 1668 the town was formally established and named Haddam, after the English village of Much Hadham, where the family of Connecticut’s first governor, John Haynes, had an estate. The first settlement stood on the west bank. Early residents grew rye, corn and grass and raised cattle, and for the town’s first two hundred years the river was both transportation and income.

The town spreads out

In 1685 a group of residents settled East Haddam, which became a separate town in 1734. Haddam Neck was settled around 1712 and stayed part of Haddam. Shipyards were built along both banks, and the small brooks and streams feeding the river powered mills and factories.

The Revolutionary War

Haddam served as a provision town, supplying troops with fish, beef and pork, and in the winter of 1778 it housed Washington’s dragoons’ horses. Haddam men served in the local militia and took part in privateering, the state-sanctioned practice of capturing enemy ships.

Stone, ships and turnpikes

After the war many residents moved west to New York and Ohio as farmland wore out, and trade and industry expanded instead. Two large granite quarries employed many residents and sent curb and building stone to New York, Boston and New Orleans, while schooners, sloops and brigs built in local yards sailed in coastal and West Indies trade. In 1785, when Middlesex County was formed, Haddam and Middletown were both designated county seats, each required to build a courthouse and jail; they shared court responsibilities for over a hundred years. The courts moved entirely to Middletown in the 1880s, but the jail in Haddam stayed active until the early 1970s. In 1802 the Middlesex Turnpike opened through town as the main overland route between Hartford and the shoreline, and in 1871 the Connecticut Valley Railroad arrived, giving Haddam five stations from Goodspeed’s to Higganum.

Industry in Higganum

Large-scale industry came to Higganum in the early 19th century, starting with water power. Higganum Manufacturing — later Clark Cutaway Harrow — built the Higganum Reservoir to power its factory and became one of the town’s biggest employers. From 1844, D. and H. Scovil Hoe Company used the water power of Candlewood Hill Brook to manufacture planters’ hoes, employing area residents well into the 20th century. Russell Manufacturing, on the Higganum River, produced elastic webbing, belting and suspenders. These industries brought Swedish, Irish and Italian immigrants to the community.

A changing town

By the mid-20th century most industry had moved away or scaled back and Haddam became a primarily suburban residential community — with one large exception: the Connecticut Yankee Atomic Power Plant, built in Haddam Neck in 1968, which produced more than 110 billion kilowatt hours before it closed in 1996. By the 1950s most residents were working and shopping outside town, in Middletown, Hartford or New Haven. The town responded by building new schools for the regionalized school system, a fire house, a town office building and a transfer station, and by renovating and reusing older buildings for services and programs. Haddam is home to seven houses of worship, many of them long-established.

Venture Smith: a Haddam Neck life

One resident of Haddam deserves particular mention. Venture Smith was born Broteer Furro in 1729, the eldest son of a West African prince, and spent his early years in what is now Guinea. At six years old he was captured by an enemy tribe, sold into slavery and sent to New England, where his first owner changed his name to Venture.

He worked under three different masters for more than thirty years before purchasing his own freedom for 71 pounds and 2 shillings — an enormous sum for the time, enough to buy hundreds of acres. Free, he took the surname Smith in honor of his final owner and built a new life as a successful fisherman, farmer, businessman and landowner in Haddam Neck, where he lived from 1775 until 1805. He was able to purchase the freedom of his wife and children as well.

The town’s own account describes Venture Smith’s story of hard work, tenacity and ingenuity as a role model for Haddam and its residents.

How the town is governed

Haddam operates under the selectman–town meeting form of government, with a Board of Selectmen and a town meeting of residents. Boards and commissions — among them the Park & Recreation Commission, the Economic Development Commission, the Planning and Zoning bodies and the Higganum Cove Advisory Committee — meet on posted schedules.

Town offices are at the Town Office Building, 30 Field Park Drive, Haddam, CT 06438; the main phone number is 860-345-8531.
